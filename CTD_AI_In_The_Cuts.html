How runtime was measured: the base doesn't store a master-duration field, but every AI clip carries its timeline Seq-Out timecode on a broadcast master that starts at 01:00:00:00. The last AI clip in each cut therefore marks elapsed program time. Because a program can run a little past its final AI shot, these runtimes are a floor — so the true AI share is these figures or slightly lower.

What it says

  • AI is a consistent ~28–33% of every episode. Across three independent cuts on three different stories, roughly a third of the finished program is generative-AI footage — this is a structural share of the show, not a one-off.
  • 101 and 102 are neck-and-neck at ~33% despite one being a locked fine cut and the other a first rough cut — the AI load doesn't shrink as a cut matures; the fine cut of 101 actually carries slightly more AI than its own earlier rough cut (RC2 was 11:28, the fine cut is 14:23).
  • 103 is lower at 27.9% — expected for a first assembly with more sequences still to fill; it will likely rise toward the others as re-spins and new shots land.

Yes — we're getting more efficient, sharply. The generate-to-use ratio has more than halved across three episodes: 101 needed ~5 generations per clip that made the cut, 102 dropped to 3, and 103 is down to 2.4. Same output (a third of each episode is AI) for less than half the generation waste. That's the prompt-library locks, reference-frame workflow, and re-spin discipline compounding.
Read with two caveats: (1) 101 has been in production longest, so it has accumulated the most experimental and re-spun generations — some of its 1,879 is early-days learning that later episodes skip. (2) "Clips in cut" counts timeline placements; a clip used twice counts twice, so true distinct-clip efficiency is marginally leaner still. The direction is unambiguous regardless.
